## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Relation to prior work on rebound in water reuse

The mechanism described here is distinct from three established mechanisms that also produce adverse outcomes in water reuse, and the distinction matters because they are frequently conflated. Schwabe et al. describe a supply-side self-consumption: indoor conservation reduces wastewater collection, which shrinks the reclaimed pool available downstream. Tran et al. describe quality-side deterioration: drought combined with conservation raises influent concentration and degrades effluent quality. Ours is a blend-side penalty: both sources meet their quality standards, the pool is large enough, and yet raising the blending ratio alone reduces the cycles of concentration and raises make-up demand. The three can occur independently, and only the third is invisible to volume-based accounting.

Against assessments of the global potential of data–water symbiosis, our result is complementary rather than corrective. Wang et al. quantified, across 4775 data centres and 57,547 wastewater plants, savings of roughly 1.3 billion m³ of water and 84 million tonnes of CO₂-equivalent per year, plus bidirectional heat recovery. We do not dispute that estimate; we identify the ratio at which its marginal saving begins to be eroded by the water-quality penalty. Their question is how much can be saved, ours is when saving stops being saving. Two asymmetries must be stated rather than glossed. Their evidence base, a global inventory with life-cycle assessment and optimisation, is stronger than ours, so nothing here should be read as a correction. And they include bidirectional heat recovery, returning data-centre waste heat to sludge drying and anaerobic digestion, a coupling that our model omits entirely by treating data-centre heat as waste. That omission is a dimension we lack.

Against facility-scale accounting the relation is one of level rather than of disagreement. Reviews of data-centre water use conclude that no single formula applies and that the optimum depends on the local combination of factors, and national inventories have established the magnitude of the demand, including the concentration of Chinese compute in water-scarce regions. Those studies answer how much water is used. Ours answers which constraint fails first and how raising the reclaimed ratio reorders that sequence. The specific value added lies in a mechanism that facility-scale accounting cannot express: water-footprint accounting treats water as a homogeneous commodity, so the effect of blend salinity on the cycles of concentration is summed away. Field measurements on a 4.2 MW tower report that raising the cycles of concentration improves water efficiency, which is the same mechanism at a different scale. We therefore claim directional agreement and not agreement with measurement: one facility in the oil and gas sector is not a city-scale model.

### 4.2 Three levers that must not be conflated

A practical corollary follows from separating the effect of the substitution ratio from the effects of capacity and production, because all three are commonly described as expanding reclaimed-water use and they point in different directions. Expanding treatment capacity while leaving production unchanged is ineffective: capacity is not the binding quantity, and the marginal capacity gain is identically zero across the whole scanned range at both endpoints. Raising reclaimed-water production, by increasing the wastewater diversion fraction from 0.16 to 0.25, is effective and relocates the bottleneck from source abstraction to grid connection at the water-scarce endpoint, a gain of 300 MW on the grid used for that comparison. Raising the substitution ratio is harmful once $r>r^{*}$, because it amplifies demand through the quality side. Capacity expansion does not change available production, production expansion adds supply, and ratio expansion inflates demand; conflating them yields planning conclusions of the opposite sign.

Two mechanisms can make the policy constraint fail, and they must be reported separately. The reclaimed pool may be capacity-limited, in which case reclaimed use saturates and the constraint fails because of a hard supply ceiling. Or the water-quality penalty may dilute the denominator, so that the same pool corresponds to a larger make-up demand; at the water-scarce endpoint and 1600 MW reclaimed use rises only from 25,157 to 25,567, or 1.6%, while total withdrawal rises 5.4%. Both operate, but the second dominates at the margin, and it is the new mechanism. Treating the first as the whole story would misplace the conclusion.

### 4.3 What the criterion is good for

A reviewer may reasonably ask what policy value a mechanism has when it does not operate in the current policy range. The answer is that the criterion does not forecast any city's present state; it delimits an interval, and the interval is useful at every stage. For a city far below $r^{*}$ it supplies an executable planning ceiling: the ratio can be raised to $r^{*}$ without cost, which is a stronger statement than saying that a higher ratio happens to be harmless for now. For a city near $r^{*}$ it supplies the location of the threshold and its parameter sensitivity, since a 10% error in either $L$ or $\mathrm{CoC}_d$ moves $r^{*}$ by 0.05 to 0.09. For a city beyond $r^{*}$ it supplies the direction and magnitude of the penalty. For a city planning a fully reclaimed campus it supplies the four-regime classification, which answers whether such a target is achievable at all, and the classification requires only four parameters. This is more useful than asking whether reclaimed-water cooling is beneficial, because the answer to that question depends on the city while the criterion makes the dependence explicit. What the criterion does not supply is advice: reading this paper as a recommendation that a particular city set its target at 55% would exceed the evidence, which rests on synthetic endpoints.

One methodological consequence deserves separate mention, because it is where volume-based policy indicators lose information. City-wide reclaimed-water utilisation has total wastewater throughput as its denominator, so a city can report 25% utilisation while a single campus consumes that entire 25% and operates at a campus ratio near 100%, in which case the penalty is fully triggered; conversely, if the same volume is spread across a hundred small users, every ratio stays low and no penalty occurs. At the modelled 1000 MW scale the ratio of wastewater throughput to campus make-up is 5.28, so a city-wide utilisation of 20% already permits a campus ratio above 1.0. Aggregate utilisation therefore conceals exactly the heterogeneity that the mechanism depends on, which is consistent with the broader point that bottleneck location is governed by structure rather than by total volume.

### 4.4 Limitations

The dominant limitation is that nothing here is calibrated to a real city. All capacity numbers are conditional on synthetic 730-day drivers, a single parameter set per endpoint, and ten in-model constraint thresholds that have not been calibrated against any city, so they are demonstrations of mechanism rather than estimates of reality. The two endpoints are corners of a parameter domain, and a 25-city gradient scan along the parameter axes was used only to check continuity, not to support any multi-city claim. The design cycles of concentration, which controls the threshold, carries no anchor in our parameter envelope, which makes the classification boundary the weakest link in the argument.

The bottleneck attribution rests on a single deterministic scan with no random source, so no sampling error estimate exists for the reported dominant constraint. A $\pm2\%$ perturbation of all thresholds moves the daily capacity by up to 250 MW at the water-scarce endpoint, roughly 75 MW per 1% of threshold, so absolute capacities are unreliable; what survives is the migration structure, with the water-scarce endpoint's first failure in the range 1000 to 1350 MW and the cool endpoint's at 1450 to 1500 MW, two non-overlapping intervals. The grid resolution of 50 MW adds quantisation, which is why only the zero-effect region and the endpoint decline are cited as numbers. Intervention interactions were not tested, since the nine interventions were applied one at a time.

The semantics of the grid constraint are unresolved. The model does not distinguish campus-connection capacity from node-available capacity in the urban grid, and that distinction governs how the electrical bottleneck should be read in practice. It may represent an engineering constraint on campus interconnection rather than physical headroom in the city grid, and this is the element most likely to be challenged. Resolving it requires at least one real node's seasonal load curve.

The threshold value is not a universal constant. It is the value at the endpoint water-quality parameters, and any city's value must be computed from its own. The classification boundaries are thin: when $C^{*}$ approaches either source salinity, a small parameter error changes the regime assignment, so we claim a classification method rather than any city's classification. Regime I was verified with a design value of 15, which is engineeringly unrealistic since real towers rarely exceed 8, and that construction serves logical verification rather than description. The regime validation itself used a single endpoint, a single capacity and five substitution ratios, so its universality rests on the analytic derivation rather than on exhaustive simulation.

The real-city section is an application and not a verification, and the distinction is not rhetorical. We have not observed make-up demand rising as a city crossed $r^{*}$; that would require facility-level time series we do not hold, and treating that section as confirmation would exceed the evidence. The two documented shares have different denominators and cannot be summed, and their reporting period is undisclosed. The city-wide ratio is an average over 56 facilities rather than a property of any one, and the estimate of $k$ is sensitive to caliber: the supply-capacity and actual-use calibers differ by 5.5 percentage points in $r$, which is the same magnitude as the 5.4-point margin to the threshold, so the direction of the conclusion is determined while its size is not. The state-wide distribution of $k$ is drawn from a selected subsample and cannot be extrapolated to the state's water systems.

Finally, the time-scale results are deliberately demoted rather than argued. Annual boundaries exceed daily ones by 500 and 250 MW at the two endpoints and the hourly proxy falls below by 50 and 100 MW, but the hourly proxy is built from a synthesised intraday load curve with assumed training, inference and peak factors, not from measured hourly load. We report its existence and direction and do not use it as evidence for the main claim.

## 5. Conclusions

- A closed-form threshold, $r^{*}=(L/\mathrm{CoC}_d-C_p)/(C_r-C_p)$, determines the reclaimed-water substitution ratio above which raising the ratio increases total cooling make-up demand; it matches model output to machine precision and requires only four water-quality parameters.
- The response is threshold-typed. The penalty is exactly zero for $r\le r^{*}$ and becomes independent of data-centre capacity beyond it, with a $+6.47$ to $+6.48\%$ increase in total withdrawal between 200 and 1600 MW.
- Bottleneck location is an endogenous function of the policy variable. Under a purely physical constraint set the water-scarce endpoint loses about 100 MW as the ratio rises from 0.5 to 0.9, while the grid constraint remains unchanged; the cool endpoint loses essentially nothing because its bottleneck is electrical.
- Two conditions must hold together for the penalty to change carrying capacity: the ratio must exceed $r^{*}$, and the first-failing constraint must lie on the water side. The cool endpoint is the negative result that establishes the second condition.
- Within current policy ranges the mechanism has no capacity consequence. This exact zero is reproduced on 50, 25 and 10 MW grids and is falsifiable by any non-zero observation, which makes it a stronger claim than a predicted small effect.
- The criterion reduces to a screen requiring two published numbers. Cities whose ratio of potable to reclaimed water use falls below 3.50 necessarily exceed $r^{*}$; among 169 California systems, 13.6% fall below this value.
- Three levers must not be conflated. Expanding reclaimed-water treatment capacity does not move the bottleneck, raising reclaimed-water production does, and raising the substitution ratio is counterproductive beyond $r^{*}$.
- The results demonstrate mechanism under synthetic parameters and are not estimates of any city's carrying capacity; calibration against a city with a published water balance and at least one metered campus remains the step required to convert the criterion into a forecast.
