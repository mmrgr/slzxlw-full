A B S T R A C T
The global importance of data center water use is increasing with the rapid growth of digitalization and artificial 
intelligence. This study analyzes the factors influencing workload-level water use, measured in liters consumed 
per workload, to guide water-saving strategies in data centers. Our findings reveal workload-level water use 
variations exceeding 10,000-fold, driven by over 1000-fold differences in water consumption per kilowatt hour 
of server electricity consumed and approximately 10-fold differences in server workload efficiency. Key de­
terminants are ranked as server efficiency, electrical grid water consumption factors, server utilization, cooling 
system type, infrastructure efficiency, climate zone, inactive server percentage, and server refresh cycle. Notably, 
there is no single recipe for minimizing water use; instead, optimal outcomes depend on tailored combinations of 
these factors. This analysis addresses critical knowledge gaps by identifying the determinants of data center 
water use and exploring their achievable minima under diverse site-specific constraints.
1. Introduction
Increasing demand for digital services has led to an increased reli­
ance on data centers—the backbone of the internet which provides the 
necessary functions of data processing, communication, and storage 
(Shehabi et al., 2024, 2016). However, the operation of data centers 
requires significant amounts of electricity and water (Masanet et al., 
2020; Mytton, 2021). While data center electricity use has been the 
subject of much investigation (Andrae and Edler, 2015; Belkhir and 
Elmeligi, 2018; Jones, 2018; N. Lei and Masanet, 2021; Masanet et el., 
2020), data center water consumption is a less studied topic that is 
becoming increasingly important in the context of water scarcity and 
freshwater conservation, especially at local levels (Lei and Masanet, 
2022; Mytton, 2021; M.A.B. Siddik et al., 2021). Many data centers can 
consume a significant amount of water directly for cooling and space 
conditioning (Lei and Masanet, 2022). For example, Google’s data 
centers in The Dalles consumed 355 million gallons of water in 2021, 
representing 29 % of the city’s total water consumption, which has 
raised concerns about local water stress, particularly in light of the 
recent drought conditions in this region (Oregon Tech, 2022). Addi­
tionally, data centers consume water indirectly through the water used 
for electricity generation (Mytton 2021; Shehabi et al., 2016; Siddik 
et al., 2024, M.A.B. 2021). This type of water use can be significant at 
the local or national level depending on the power generation technol­
ogies, power mixes, and electricity usage of the data centers. For 
example, some very large data centers have power capacities of hun­
dreds of megawatts and the generation of 1 kW-hour of thermoelec­
tricity or hydropower could consume 0.18–2.0 liters or 0.67–1194 liters 
of water (Lee et al., 2018), respectively. This demonstrates a significant 
amount of data center indirect water use and further emphasizes the 
importance of considering data center water use going forward.

Conclusion
Increasing digitalization and freshwater restrictions have brought 
data center water conservation into the spotlight. While more data 
center operators are beginning to track and report WUE-site and WUE- 
source, these metrics fall short in measuring water use at the work­
load level, which is the ideal basis for understanding and comparing 
water use in data centers.
This study focuses on analyzing the determinants of overall 
workload-level water use, enabling more direct comparisons across data 
centers with diverse site-specific characteristics. Using statistical anal­
ysis of recent server performance data, we first highlighted the rela­
tionship between server electricity efficiency, utilization levels, refresh 
rates, and inactive server management as determinants of direct server 
electricity use per workload. Next, we examined the relationship be­
tween PUE and WUE-site through climate- and technology-specific 
simulations of major data center cooling archetypes, analyzing their 
influence on facility-level electricity and water use per workload. 
Finally, we assessed the relationship between electrical WCFs, WUE- 
source, and overall water use per workload. In general, this paper ad­
dresses critical gaps in existing research and contributes in the following
