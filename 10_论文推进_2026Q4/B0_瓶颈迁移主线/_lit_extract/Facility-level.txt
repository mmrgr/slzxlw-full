A B S T R A C T
Data centers have rapidly expanded to meet increasing computing demands. As energy-intensive infrastructures, 
data centers consume significant amounts of water to meet cooling requirements and support operations. Here, 
we present a bottom-up framework to estimate the facility-level water footprints (WFs) of data centers in China 
and project future pathways. First, we compile an inventory of Chinese data center operations and calculate both 
direct and indirect water consumption. Then, by mapping water scarcity levels, we assess the dependence of the 
data centers on limited water supplies. Finally, 21 scenarios are developed to model WFs trends and water 
scarcity implications from 2025 to 2050. The results show that in 2022, China’s data centers consumed 15.7 
billion m3 of water, accounting for 2.7 % of the nation’s total. Notably, 72 % of the computing capacity is located 
in severe water-scarce regions. By 2050, simultaneous improvements in power usage efficiency, water usage 
efficiency, and grid decarbonization could reduce national WFs by 51.5–58.3 % compared with the baseness as 
usual scenario, alleviating the increased water consumption driven by the Eastern Data and Western Computing 
Project in water-scarce regions such as Gansu and Ningxia Provinces. This study fills a gap in understanding the 
water consumption of Chinese data centers, highlighting the urgent need to incorporate water resource limita­
tions into planning for sustainable digital infrastructure.

1. Introduction
The rapid expansion of the information technology (IT) industry has 
driven explosive growth in the demand for supporting infrastructure. By 
2024, the global IT market is projected to reach a global value of 344 
billion USD [1]. Data centers serve as the information backbone of the 
increasingly digitalized world, housing IT equipment, such as servers, 
storage devices, and network hardware, alongside systems to meet their 
power and cooling demands [2]. With over 11,000 data centers oper­
ating worldwide [3], their expansion is increasingly linked to the 
widespread adoption of artificial intelligence, machine learning, and 
other data-intensive technologies [4] [5].
As essential infrastructure in the digital era, data centers are also 
significant consumers of electricity and water [6]. The continuous 
operation of servers and cooling systems contributes to approximately 
1–2 % of global electricity use [7,8]. Their operations also lead to both 
direct and indirect water consumption. Data centers consume water

Conclusion
This study presents the first bottom-up assessment of water foot­
prints for Chinese data centers, capturing both direct onsite water use 
and indirect water use linked to electricity consumption. We map the 
geographic dependence of data centers on water-stressed basins, project 
water-footprint trajectories from 2025 to 2050, and evaluate how shifts 
in data center deployment may exacerbate regional water scarcity under 
future climate scenarios.
We found that China’s data centers consumed 15.7 billion m3 of 
water in 2022, with 72 % of computing capacity located in regions 
suffering from severe water scarcity. Under a business-as-usual trajec­
tory, the Eastern Data and Western Computing Project would further 
increase water demand in arid provinces such as Gansu and Ningxia. 
However, by 2050, simultaneous improvements in power usage effi­
ciency, water usage efficiency, and grid decarbonization could cut na­
tional water footprints by 51.5–58.3 %, offsetting the additional 
pressure from large-scale computing migration.
Although this study focuses on China, the bottom-up framework and 
scenario-based evaluation approach developed here can be applied to 
other regions. Future studies can adopt our modelling structure in 
different geopolitical contexts, such as Western Europe or North Amer­
ica. Such cross-regional applications would allow the identification of 
globally transferable best practices and help inform international data 
center siting and water resource management strategies.
