a b s t r a c t
Water sensitive interventions are being promoted to reduce the adverse impacts of urban development
on natural water cycles. However it is currently difﬁcult to know the best strategy for their imple-
mentation because current and desired urban water performance is not well quantiﬁed. This is partic-
ularly at the city-region scale, which is important for strategic urban planning. This work aimed to ﬁll this
gap by quantifying the water performance of urban systems within city-regions using ‘urban water
metabolism’ evaluation, to inform decisions about water sensitive interventions. To do this we adapted
an existing evaluation framework with new methods. In particular, we used land use data for deﬁning
system boundaries, and for estimating natural hydrological ﬂows. The criteria for gauging the water
performance were water efﬁciency (in terms of water extracted externally) and hydrological perfor-
mance (how much natural hydrological ﬂows have changed relative to a nominated pre-urbanised state).
We compared these performance criteria for urban systems within three Australian city-regions (South
East Queensland, Melbourne and Perth metropolitan areas), under current conditions, and after imple-
mentation of example water sensitive interventions (demand management, rainwater/stormwater har-
vesting, wastewater recycling and increasing perviousness). The respective water efﬁciencies were found
to be 79, 90 and 133 kL/capita/yr. In relation to hydrological performance, stormwater runoff relative to
pre-urbanised ﬂows was of most note, estimated to be 2-, 6- and 3- fold, respectively. The estimated
performance beneﬁts from water sensitive interventions suggested different priorities for each region,
and that combined implementation of a range of interventions may be necessary to make substantive
gains in performance. We concluded that the framework is suited to initial screening of the type and
scale of water sensitive interventions needed to achieve desired water performance objectives.

1. Introduction
Innovations in total water cycle management are being pro-
moted to secure water supplies and reduce the impacts of urban
development on natural water cycles (ADB, 2016, OECD, 2015, UK
Water Partnership, 2015), and articulated in concepts such as
‘water-sensitive cities’ (Wong and Brown, 2009) and ‘water wise
cities’ (IWA, 2016). These include water resource management in-
terventions such as improved water use efﬁciency and diversiﬁca-
tion of supplies (harvesting of rainwater and stormwater runoff,
wastewater recycling) (Campisano et al., 2017; Khastagir and
Jayasuriya, 2010; Sharma et al., 2013), and planning interventions
such as water sensitive urban design (WSUD) and greater green
space (Coutts et al., 2013; Feng et al., 2016; Sharma et al., 2016). We
collectively refer to these as water-sensitive interventions.
To understand how best to employ these interventions, water
managers and urban planners need quantiﬁcation of urban water
performance (i.e., how efﬁciency water is being utilised, how the
natural water cycle is altered) (Chesterﬁeld et al., 2016). They need

--- CONCLUSIONS ---
Conclusions
We took a big-picture perspective to the evaluation of the urban
water performance, by extending an urban water metabolism
evaluation framework to assess urban systems within three
Australia city-regions. To test how it can inform water management
decisions, we evaluated these regions under current conditions and
after the implementation of example water sensitive interventions.
Water performance was evaluated in terms of the external water
efﬁciency and the hydrological performance of the urban system.
Adapting the evaluation framework to a large urban scale
required the development of new methods for i) deﬁning the sys-
tem boundaries using land use data; and ii) for estimating natural
hydrological ﬂows by combining the land use data with hydrolog-
ical ﬂow partitioning (HFP) factors. The method can be conducted
relatively rapidly, and could be used by water managers and urban
planners for strategic evaluation. It is transferrable to other city-
regions if spatial land use data are available, along with data for
water supply and wastewater generation that can be aligned to the
deﬁned urban system boundaries.
We found that application of the evaluation framework to urban
systems at the larger urban scale is suited to initial screening of the
type and scale of water sensitive interventions needed to achieve
desired water performance objectives, and to guide the establish-
ment of performance objectives and targets. The values of the
improved framework have been the broader urban perspective (to
align with and inform strategic urban and water planning pro-
cesses), and the systematic deﬁnition of the urban system bound-
ary (to enable consistent comparisons between urban systems and
over time). The framework can enable a better understanding of the
role and impact of water sensitive interventions in achieving water
performance objectives for urban systems within city-regions.
