use4–12. Similar to what has been observed in irrigated agriculture, 
these efforts have led to increases in water-use efficiency and a level-
ling off of water use in many municipalities worldwide. For exam-
ple, between 1995 and 2010, daily average water use per capita in 
California’s urban sector decreased by nearly 30%, which was largely 
due to programs that have encouraged, required or incentivized the 
adoption of lower-water-use technologies13.
On the supply side of the ledger, municipalities are increasingly 
augmenting their water-supply portfolios with treated municipal 
wastewater (TMW). In the United States, municipal wastewater 
reuse—which supplies agricultural and urban irrigation, industrial 
processes and potable water use—increased from 4 million m3 d−1 in 
1995 to nearly 8.5 million m3 d−1 in 2015 (ref. 14). In California, reuse 
of TMW for urban uses increased from 34 million m3 to more than 
313 million m3 annually from 1970 to 2015, even outpacing agricul-
tural reuse15. Owing to the cost and reliability advantages that are 
associated with TMW, such trends will probably continue16,17. The 
National Research Council estimates that TMW has the potential to 
provide up to 27% of the overall water supplies of US municipalities, 
up from its current 5% (ref. 18). California alone plans to increase 
recycled water use by nearly 500%—from 521 million m3 to more 
than 3,083 million m3 per year —by 2029 (ref. 15).
However, a more careful examination of the potential synergis-
tic effects among some types of conservation and reuse exposes a 
conflict. By invoking basic water-balance accounting19,20, it is clear 
that indoor water conservation may reduce the quantity and qual-
ity of wastewater that is available for reuse (Fig. 1). The extent to 
which reductions in water use impact the generation and quality of 
wastewater has yet to be explored systematically, except for one-off 
analyses of single plants14. The potential impacts of declining flows 
within an interconnected water system can be broad, including 
reducing the availability of treated wastewater for irrigated agricul-
ture, golf courses, groundwater recharge or critical flows to local 
effluent-dominated streams and rivers that provide habitat and rec-
reational services. Owing to the coevolution and interdependencies 
of factors that comprise our complex human water systems21–25, such 
impacts can even result in increased water scarcity and necessitate 
additional water reduction requests and measures.

Our results contribute to a broader body of literature showing 
that the failure of policy to consider the interdependencies and 
coevolution of factors that comprise a human-water system will lead 
to unanticipated water scarcity and unintended consequences19–25, 
consequences that will be borne by the environment, the users of 
recycled water, water managers and, therefore, society as a whole. 
Diligence in incorporating water balance is one ingredient to help to 
avoid such unanticipated and unintended outcomes, as is an under-
standing that lowering the costs of water use (for example, through 
improvements in technical efficiency) will often lead to produc-
tion or behavioural changes that result in increased water use and 
greater levels of scarcity absent some sort of rationing. Recognition
