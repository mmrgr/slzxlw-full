## 1. Introduction

Data-centre water use has become a new variable in urban water management. Most existing assessments operate at the facility scale: water use effectiveness per unit of information-technology power, comparisons between evaporative and dry cooling, and site-level water-risk scoring. Such accounting answers how much water a given facility withdraws, but it cannot answer the planning question that a city actually faces. When compute capacity grows from 100 MW to 1000 MW, does water quantity, water-treatment capacity, wastewater capacity or grid connection fail first? And which investment would genuinely raise the feasible scale? Neither answer follows from the absolute size of the facility; both follow from the city's own residual-margin structure. A water-rich city with a thin grid and a water-scarce city with a generous grid fail in entirely different places.

The reclaimed-water substitution ratio, defined here as the share of reclaimed water in the cooling make-up stream of a data-centre campus, is treated throughout this literature as a free policy variable. The implicit argument for reclaimed-water cooling is that a higher substitution ratio is always better: it displaces potable water one-for-one and therefore saves water. This step is where the reasoning becomes incomplete. The cycles of concentration of a cooling tower are bounded by the salinity of the blended make-up stream. Because reclaimed water is more saline than potable water, raising the substitution ratio raises make-up salinity, which depresses the permissible cycles of concentration; and the cycles of concentration determine make-up demand per unit of heat rejected. Raising the substitution ratio can therefore increase total make-up demand while decreasing potable abstraction. Two strands of work gesture at this possibility. Field measurements on a reclaimed-water-cooled tower report that raising the cycles of concentration improves water efficiency, and a recent perspective argues that the volumetric paradigm is exhausted and that the frontier is water-quality compatibility. Neither provides a computable criterion. The literature has not answered how high the substitution ratio must rise before the penalty begins, nor tested whether that penalty relocates the system bottleneck.

Three further gaps follow from the same facility-level framing. First, facility-scale metrics do not map onto system bottlenecks: a technology that halves water use per unit of power may deliver zero additional feasible capacity in a city whose binding constraint is the grid. Second, distinct estimands are routinely reported as a single number. The physical carrying boundary, the policy-compliance threshold, the annual-scale boundary, the daily-scale boundary and the hourly proxy boundary are five different quantities, and conflating them invites a policy target to be read as a physical limit. Third, interventions are rarely subjected to a counterfactual test. In a city whose reclaimed pool is already committed to incumbent users, the effect of a data centre drawing reclaimed water depends on whether it takes new supply or reallocated stock, and those two have opposite signs in the city water ledger.

Here we take the position of a mechanism study. Because calibration data for a real city are unavailable, we claim no city's carrying capacity and no city's threshold value. What we deliver is a criterion, the ordering and migration structure of constraints, and the conditions under which the mechanism binds. Our contributions are five. (i) We derive a closed-form water-quality compatibility threshold, $r^{*}=(L/\mathrm{CoC}_d-C_p)/(C_r-C_p)$, which states how high the substitution ratio may rise before the penalty begins, given the loop salinity limit, the design cycles of concentration, and the salinities of the two sources; it agrees with model output to machine precision. (ii) We show that the response is threshold-typed rather than gradual: for $r\le r^{*}$ the carrying capacity and total withdrawal are exactly unchanged, and deterioration begins only beyond $r^{*}$. This is not a small effect; it is an effect that is zero and then kinks. (iii) We show that the bottleneck location is an endogenous function of the policy variable: under a purely physical constraint set the water-scarce endpoint loses about 100 MW as $r$ rises, its first-failing constraint moves forward, and its water-side ratios deteriorate while the grid constraint does not move at all. (iv) We state the two conditions under which the penalty changes carrying capacity, namely a water-quality penalty and a water-side bottleneck, and confirm the second condition by a negative result at the cool endpoint, where the penalty occurs but has no capacity consequence. (v) We separate three levers that are routinely conflated: expanding reclaimed-water treatment capacity is ineffective, raising reclaimed-water production is effective and migrates the bottleneck, and raising the substitution ratio is harmful beyond $r^{*}$.

Two scope statements belong here rather than in the limitations. We claim no capacity loss under current policy. In the range $r\le0.35$, which contains current Chinese reclaimed-water utilisation targets, the carrying capacity of both endpoints does not vary with $r$ at all, and this exact zero is identical on all three capacity grids we tested. A linear mechanism cannot produce an exact zero; a threshold mechanism can, and that zero is falsifiable by any non-zero observation. The policy value of the criterion therefore lies in delimiting a safe interval rather than in forecasting any city's present condition. Second, the ratio used here is the campus substitution ratio, whose denominator is campus cooling make-up; it is not the city-wide reclaimed-water utilisation rate, whose denominator is municipal wastewater throughput. The two are not interchangeable, and the bridge between them is $r_{\max}=u\,(W/D)$.

## 2. Material and methods

### 2.1 Model structure and closure

We built a daily urban water metabolism model with six component classes: source water (RES1), water treatment works (WTW1), distribution mains (DM1), wastewater treatment works (WWTW1), a central reclaimed-water facility (CENTRAL_REUSE) and the artificial-intelligence data centre (AI_DC1). Every daily step is checked against two closure identities, a storage balance and a delivery balance; across 45 scenarios and both identities all checks pass, with a maximum relative error of $2.5\times10^{-13}\%$.

The data-centre component resolves information-technology load, cooling (evaporative, dry and hybrid), make-up demand and blowdown. Potable and reclaimed water are blended to a target substitution ratio $r$, and water quality is tracked for two species, total dissolved solids (TDS) and chloride. The cooling constraint is the tighter of the two species. We report two species because that is what the current parameter set configures: the quality-limit table contains TDS (2200 mg L$^{-1}$) and chloride (500 mg L$^{-1}$) and the source-water records carry the same two. Earlier project documentation described five species; the present configuration does not, and we report the configuration that exists.

### 2.2 Parameter-domain endpoints

Two endpoints represent corners of the parameter domain rather than real cities. The water-scarce, high-cooling-load endpoint (D-HA) combines a high wet-bulb climate with a tight abstraction cap, treatment works of 370 ML d$^{-1}$, wastewater works of 270 ML d$^{-1}$ and a reclaimed facility of 40 ML d$^{-1}$. The cool-climate endpoint (D-CO) combines a low cooling load with a generous abstraction cap, 380, 270 and 36 ML d$^{-1}$ respectively. Their policy substitution targets differ (0.65 and 0.40), which is a property of the city rather than of the model, and this is precisely why the joint and purely physical constraint sets diverge at the two endpoints.

### 2.3 Constraint sets and estimands

Ten constraints are evaluated at every capacity point (Table 1). Capacity-type constraints are written against a planning-margin threshold of 0.95 rather than 1.0. This is deliberate: the engine hard-truncates treatment utilisation, so a threshold of 1.0 would make the constraint vacuously true and unable to serve as a bottleneck criterion. The 0.95 threshold asks how much margin remains, not whether the plant physically overflows. One candidate indicator, the reclaimed-plant utilisation ratio, is excluded: the facility carries a 300 ML storage pond, roughly seven to nine days of production, so reaching nameplate treatment rate on a daily step is normal buffering rather than failure, and the indicator is identically 1.0 across the whole capacity range, which would drive every carrying capacity to zero.

| # | Constraint | Operator | Threshold |

| 2 | Domestic unmet demand | $\le$ | 0 |
| 3 | Cooling water unmet | $\le$ | 0 |
| 4 | Maximum WTW utilisation | $\le$ | 0.95 |
| 5 | Maximum WWTW utilisation | $\le$ | 0.95 |
| 6 | Maximum source abstraction ratio | $\le$ | 0.95 |
| 7 | Reclaimed substitution ratio | $\ge$ | 0.65 / 0.40 |
| 8 | Peak capacity ratio | $\le$ | 1.0 |
| 9 | Maximum local connection ratio | $\le$ | 1.0 |
| 10 | Maximum daily-average grid connection ratio | $\le$ | 1.0 |

Constraint 7 is the policy constraint. We therefore report two constraint sets throughout: a joint set that includes it, and a purely physical set that excludes it. The distinction is not a technical detail but a premise of the argument. Our independent variable is the substitution target $r$ itself; were we to leave the condition that delivered ratio must meet target ratio inside the criterion set, raising $r$ would necessarily make failure more likely, which is self-reference rather than physical evidence. All conclusions about whether $r$ changes carrying capacity therefore rest on the purely physical set. The joint set appears only as a contrast, and the gap between the two must always be stated with it; at the water-scarce endpoint the gap reaches 300 MW at $r=0.9$.

Because the same model yields several distinct boundary values, every capacity number in this paper is tagged with its estimand: daily or annual aggregation, joint or purely physical constraint set, and a separate hourly proxy. The annual estimand answers what is feasible when policy and physics must both hold; the physical annual estimand answers where the physical system would bind if the policy target were not a constraint. These are not stricter and looser versions of one number; they answer different questions. Full definitions of all six estimands and of the three counterfactuals they serve are given in SI Section S1.

### 2.4 Scan protocol and bottleneck attribution

Capacity is scanned from 0 to 2000 MW in 50 MW steps, 41 points in total, with the full 730-day model re-run at every point. The first-failure capacity is the first point at which at least one constraint is violated; the maximum safe capacity is the last point before it. The 0 MW point serves as the no-AI baseline for counterfactual differencing.

When several constraints fail at the same capacity point, the dominant constraint is the one with the largest dimensionless violation, computed as violation divided by the absolute target, with ties broken by constraint name. The scale-free measure is required because constraints carry different units: at the water-scarce endpoint the domestic-unmet constraint first fails at 1850 MW with a margin of 167.78 ML, whereas the abstraction ratio first fails at 1200 MW with a margin of only 0.00495 in dimensionless terms. Ranking by raw margin would place domestic supply ahead of the true bottleneck. The rule also makes the reported bottleneck independent of the insertion order of the constraint dictionary, which is a precondition for reproducibility.

Two further protocol decisions must be stated. First, intervention counterfactuals were generated on a 100 MW grid while the main results use 50 MW, so an intervention gain may only be differenced against a baseline from the same grid. We therefore re-ran every intervention on the 50 MW grid, and all gains reported here share the grid of the main results; the earlier 100 MW values differed by up to one grid step and are superseded. Second, capacity grids introduce quantisation. We re-ran the decisive comparisons at 25 MW and 10 MW resolution, and the outcome of that test governs which numbers may be cited and with what tolerance.

### 2.5 Parameter anchoring and what is not calibrated

External anchoring covers parameter ranges and orders of magnitude only; it is never used as a calibration target. Of 54 model parameters, 10 fall inside a documented envelope, 10 sit on an envelope boundary, 32 carry no anchor at all and 2 fall outside it. The anchors themselves come from a convenience sample of the local literature library rather than a formal systematic review with a search protocol. The design cycles of concentration, which is the parameter that controls the threshold, carries no anchor in that envelope, and it is the weakest link in the classification developed below. Driver series are synthetic 730-day sequences, not observed hydrology, and no constraint threshold has been calibrated against any city.
